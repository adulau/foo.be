Here we go again. The cybersecurity community has discovered another terrifying category of tools: agentic attack frameworks. ARTEX, Cairn, PentAGI, HexStrike, and others are making headlines because they allow AI agents to automate reconnaissance, exploitation, and lateral movement. Reports of their use in real intrusions are multiplying, and defensive initiatives such as [Agentic Attack Detections][^1] are emerging to track their activities. The concerns are legitimate, but the reaction feels remarkably familiar. We seem much more comfortable discussing the dangers of tools than confronting the uncomfortable reality of the infrastructure they exploit.[^2]

In 1993, **Dan Farmer and Wietse Venema** published *Improving the Security of Your Site by Breaking Into It*.[^3] Their proposal was almost offensively simple: look at your infrastructure the way an attacker would, identify the weaknesses, and fix them. In April 1995, they released **SATAN (Security Administrator Tool for Analyzing Networks)**, making automated vulnerability discovery accessible to a much broader audience. SATAN was not particularly revolutionary in what it discovered. Its real innovation was making existing security knowledge easier to apply. Apparently, that was enough to cause a minor apocalypse in the security community.

The reactions were spectacular. SATAN was accused of putting dangerous capabilities into the wrong hands. On April 3, 1995, CERT published advisory CA-95:06, warning that the availability of an easy-to-use scanner could increase threats against vulnerable systems.[^4] Dan Farmer lost his job at Silicon Graphics shortly before the release, amid controversy surrounding the tool.[^5] Even a 1996 US Senate staff statement cited SATAN as an example of increasingly accessible hacking capabilities.[^6] The recurring argument was already there: making vulnerabilities easier to discover would somehow make the Internet more dangerous. As if the vulnerabilities had been patiently waiting for SATAN before becoming exploitable.

The irony was that **SATAN did not create vulnerabilities, and it did not even exploit them**. Farmer and Venema explicitly described a tool that identified known problems and provided guidance for fixing them.[^7] Misconfigured NFS exports, exposed services, outdated software, and broken trust relationships existed long before anyone downloaded SATAN. The underlying problem was the inability, or unwillingness, to maintain secure infrastructure. The scanner simply made that failure visible. Blaming SATAN was rather like blaming the person who switched on the lights in a collapsing building.

Fast-forward thirty-one years. Agentic attack frameworks are undeniably more capable than SATAN. They can chain actions, adapt to failures, explore alternative attack paths, and operate at speeds that make human-driven attacks look almost leisurely. Recent Google Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant research confirms that these capabilities are being used in real intrusions.[^8] This is not merely SATAN with an LLM bolted onto it. Yet the underlying targets remain depressingly familiar: vulnerable applications, forgotten services, compromised credentials, excessive privileges, and poorly maintained infrastructure. We have automated the attacker, but have we made comparable progress in maintaining what we defend?

Of course, detection matters. Understanding agentic behavior, building detection rules, and sharing indicators are necessary defensive activities. But we should be careful not to confuse detecting the latest fashionable framework with fixing the conditions that make its success possible. An abandoned server does not care whether it is scanned by SATAN, Nmap, or an autonomous AI agent. An overprivileged account does not become less dangerous because we can identify which framework abused it. And an organization unable to inventory its systems, replace obsolete dependencies, or manage its attack surface will not solve those problems by adding another collection of signatures. **We are getting better at recognizing the tools wandering through our infrastructure while remaining remarkably bad at maintaining the infrastructure itself.**

Perhaps the most striking parallel with SATAN is how easily our attention shifts toward the technology exposing or exploiting weaknesses rather than the infrastructure that allows those weaknesses to persist. In 1995, we worried about automated vulnerability scanners. In 2026, we worry about autonomous offensive agents. The difference in capability is substantial, but the underlying defensive challenge remains remarkably familiar. **We keep debating the tools capable of navigating the ruins while paying too little attention to why so much of our digital infrastructure has been allowed to become ruins in the first place.**

## Checking what we expose

Start with the addresses your team manages. The Offensive Agency gathering module I built for MISP checks those addresses against the tracker’s observations of publicly exposed agent consoles and APIs. The observation date, hostnames and ports give you something to compare with your inventory and logs.

If an address matches, check who owned it when it was observed and whether the service was meant to be public. If you confirm an unexpected exposure, work with the owner to restrict access and verify the change. I’ve written up how to set up a recurring inventory feed and look up a single IP during triage. [^1]: ThreatDefence, [*Agentic Attack Detections*](https://github.com/ThreatDefence/agentic-attack-detections), GitHub repository, 2026. An open-source collection of detection rules, hunting queries, and indicators for agentic offensive frameworks. Ironically, we are still largely stacking detection rules in GitHub repositories, hoping defenders will find, maintain, and deploy them.
